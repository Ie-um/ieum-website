Plant-Based Journeys

Korean Temple Cuisine

Korea has had a complete plant based cuisine for more than a thousand years, cooked by monks in mountain temples and shaped entirely around keeping the mind clear. It is the oldest wellness food in the country, and almost none of it is on a menu.

Food that was never trying to be healthy

Korean temple cuisine, sachal eumsik, has been cooked in mountain monasteries for well over a thousand years. It is entirely plant based, but it was never designed as a diet. It was designed to keep the mind clear enough to sit still.

No meat, no fish, and nothing to sharpen the appetite

Temple cooking leaves out meat and fish, and also the five pungent vegetables known as osinchae: garlic, onion, spring onion, chive and wild chive. They are held to stir desire and agitation, which is the opposite of what a day of meditation needs. What remains is vegetables, mountain greens, grains, and time.

Fermentation as the seasoning

With the sharp aromatics removed, flavour comes from what the temple has made itself. Soy sauce, doenjang and gochujang aged in earthenware jars in the courtyard, some of them for decades. A single spoonful of a twenty year old soy sauce carries more depth than anything a modern kitchen can build in a day.

The season decides the menu

There is no fixed carte. What is picked that week is what is cooked that week, which is why the same temple tastes entirely different in April and in November.

Where we take you

Temple food is served in a handful of places that are genuinely worth the journey, and a great many that are not. These are the ones we use.

Baekyangsa, in Jangseong

The temple where Jeong Kwan is abbess of the Cheonjinam hermitage. She has no culinary training and no restaurant, and after Netflix filmed her for Chef's Table the New York Times called her the philosopher chef. Access is limited and never guaranteed, which is exactly why it is worth having someone ask on your behalf well in advance.

Jingwansa, on the edge of Seoul

A temple inside Bukhansan National Park, close enough to reach in under an hour and remote enough that the city disappears. It runs one of the best regarded temple stays in the country and its kitchen is serious.

The Korean Temple Food Center, in Seoul

Near Jogyesa in the centre of the city, and the easiest place to cook, not only to eat. Classes run with a monastic instructor, in English, and are the right answer for a half day when a mountain is not practical.

Sanchon, in Insadong

A restaurant, not a temple, opened by a former monk, and the place that first put this food in front of visitors to Seoul. Useful as an introduction on a first evening.

What a day actually looks like

Not a tasting menu. The point is the pace.

Morning

Arrive before the heat, walk in, and sit through a short meditation or a tea. Nothing is rushed and nothing is explained at length unless you ask.

The cooking

A monastic cook shows two or three dishes from what is in season. You do the work. There is very little equipment and almost no technique to learn, which unsettles people at first and then becomes the point.

The meal

Eaten in silence, or close to it, in the room where it was made. Most people describe the same thing afterwards, which is that they ate less than usual and felt better for longer.

Afterwards

A walk, and then the car. We do not stack anything on top of this day.

Practical

When to go

Spring and autumn are best, both for the ingredients and for the walking. Summer in the mountains is humid and winter access to some hermitages is restricted.

How long

A half day in Seoul, a full day for Jingwansa, or an overnight temple stay for Baekyangsa and the southern temples.

Dietary needs

This is the one journey where they are almost never a problem. Everything is plant based already. Tell us about nut and sesame allergies and we will speak to the kitchen before you arrive.

Who it suits

People who are tired. It is also, in our experience, the journey that converts the sceptic in the group.
